\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{E1: Playback after a Peer failure}
We use 3 conditions to distinguish the effect of Peer failure on playback
from the additional effect of repair. Comparing the no-failure control (N)
with repair disabled after a Peer failure (OFF) shows how playback changes
when a Peer stops. 
Then, using OFF as the baseline, we assess the additional effect of
shared repair (S) under the same failure schedule.

Under low demand, all 6 sessions in each condition completed with zero observed post-fault stall. 
However, under high demand, 7/18 sessions completed under both OFF and S, 
compared with 18/18 under N.
Although OFF and S had the same completion count, shared repair increased
mean stall from 3.24 to 4.46\,s per viewer, a difference
of 1.22\,s.

3 capacity contexts, with matched healthy runs for N.}
\label{tab:e1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
Treatment & Sessions completed & Stall per viewer (s) \\
\midrule
N & 18/18 & 3.20 \\
OFF & 7/18 & 3.24 \\

\end{table}

Completion also depended on which Peer failed. When the slowest Peer
stopped, all 6 viewers finished under both OFF and S. In this context,
shared repair increased mean stall from 4.01 to 7.47\,s per viewer.
When the middle or fastest Peer stopped, only 1 or 0 viewers finished,
respectively, under either policy. Because each failed session stops
accumulating stall at termination, the similar stall averages under N
and OFF include very different completion outcomes.

\subsection{E2: Waiting before repair}
Under shared repair, we vary how long the coordinator waits (grace period) after it
detects a missing copy. In the brief outage, the original Peer returns
after 4\,s. While in the persistent outage, it stays offline.
Table~\ref{tab:e2} reports mean repair traffic and one-copy exposure
across the 3 failure contexts.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering\small
\caption{E2. Source repair payload per run and sampled one-copy exposure
during persistent absence.}
\label{tab:e2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}r rr r@{}}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Repair payload (MiB)} & Exposure (MiB$\cdot$s) \\
\cmidrule(lr){2-3}
Grace & Brief & Persistent & Persistent \\
\midrule
1 s & 0.125 & 0.750 & 17.42 \\
3 s & 0 & 0.750 & 18.24 \\
8 s & 0 & 0.750 & 20.20 \\

\end{table}

During each brief outage with a 1\,s grace period, the coordinator
created a repair task that copied a single 128\,KiB segment.
Once created, the task could proceed even after the original Peer returned.
With 3 or 8\,s of grace, repair generated no traffic during these runs.
All 54 brief-outage sessions completed.

During persistent outages, all 3 settings copied the same 6 segments.
Increasing grace from 1 to 8\,s raised mean one-copy exposure from 17.42
to 20.20\,MiB$\cdot$s. The additional wait therefore increased exposure
by about 16\% for the same repair volume.

\subsection{E3: Fixed and load-aware allowances}
To assess the load-aware allowance (L), we compare it with the fixed
50\% cap (F50) under both arrival patterns. Shared repair (S) and the
fixed 25\% cap (F25) provide two additional comparison points.
Each policy completed 7/18 sessions with simultaneous arrivals and 9/18
with staggered arrivals. Table~\ref{tab:e3} reports post-fault stall and
the number of runs meeting the recovery target.

stall and recovery.

Table~\ref{tab:e3-slowest} focuses on simultaneous arrivals when the slowest Peer failed.
Of the 3 failure contexts, only this one allowed all 6 viewers to finish under every policy.
Therefore, we can compare stall over complete sessions and examine how each repair policy
affected the time needed to restore the missing replicas.
In this case, L reduced mean post-fault stall by
1.65\,s per viewer relative to F50, but full replica recovery was first
verified 2.07\,s later. Viewers spent less time waiting for segments,
while one-copy exposure rose from 18.82 to 20.38\,MiB$\cdot$s.

\subsection{E4: Segment availability after a second holder failure}
We select a segment late in the repair queue and stop its original
holders in sequence. Immediately after the second stop, we request the
segment from its replacement Peer and verify the size, hash, and committed
registration. This request tests whether a viewer can fetch the segment
without relying on previously buffered data. At intervals of 4, 14,
and 30\,s between failures, all 18 requests failed.
The follow-up adds 48 trials: 30 exploratory sweep trials, 12 independent
trials at the selected 34\,s interval, and 6 completed-repair controls.
The fixed-delay follow-up horizon is 55\,s from run start; the
completion-gated controls use 75\,s. Each phase uses the same two paired
failure contexts and is reported separately.

The follow-up tested longer intervals in 30 exploratory trials
(Table~\ref{tab:e4}). At 34\,s, S and F50 each served the segment in
2/2 trials and L in 0/2. In 12 independent trials at the same delay,
S, F50, and L succeeded in 2/4, 3/4, and 4/4 trials, respectively.
The policy ordering thus changed when the comparison was repeated.

In 6 additional trials, we stopped the second holder only after every
affected segment had 2 verified, reachable, registered copies.
The remaining Peer then served all 9 segments in every trial,
passing all 54 fresh-read checks. These checks establish byte
availability after the second process failure. The stopped Peers
retained their disks.

\subsection{E5: Controller failures}
We measure Controller recovery by the first successful application request
that requires fresh, quorum-backed metadata. Each failure case has 3 runs.
When the leader stopped during delivery, the median recovery time was
1.52\,s from the stop (range 1.47--2.74\,s). Both ongoing warm sessions
and newly started uncached sessions completed in all 3 trials.
When the leader stopped during a pending repair,
the median was 1.60\,s from the stop (range 1.52--1.97\,s). In all
3 runs the new leader resumed the task with its original ID and creation
time. Subsequent checks confirmed a hash-valid copy and a committed
registration at the replacement Peer.
Further checks retained task state after a Controller restart and
the L configuration after a source-Peer restart.

During stable majority loss, all 9 valid write attempts across the 3 trials
returned HTTP 503, with no success acknowledgments.
For this case, timing begins when quorum returns. The median time to a
fresh application response was 1.04\,s (range 0.85--1.43\,s). In each
run, the Controller retained the baseline state and reconciled the
operation using its original ID.
